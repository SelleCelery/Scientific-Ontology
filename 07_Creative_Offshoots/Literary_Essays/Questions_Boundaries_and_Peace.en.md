# Keeping Questions Open Without Trampling Boundaries

An introduction to Scientific Ontology, from inquiry to peace

> Status: Literary essay
> Language: English commensurated rendering; Japanese text is authoritative
> Layer: 07_Creative_Offshoots / Literary_Essays
> Authority: A literary commensuration and contact surface, not a concept-definition owner.

## 1. Keeping Mistaken Questions Alive
### What is Scientific Ontology trying to do?

Strange ideas come to me rather easily.

When I read physics, I think of things a physicist would not entertain. When I put them to an AI, it sometimes stops me. When I go back to the sources, I sometimes find that I am more plainly wrong than I thought.

Once, I would have seen only two choices.

It was wrong, so throw it away.

Or, because I cannot bear to throw it away, defend my own interpretation instead.

These days, I do something a little more troublesome.

**Keep the question. Scale back what I claim for it.**

What does the source actually say?

How did I understand it?

What did it lead me to imagine?

Can that association really be asserted within the original field?

Has it been observed?

Is it only a structural resemblance?

Do we still know nothing about it?

As far as possible, I try not to put all these things in the same box.

As I kept doing this, I began to develop Scientific Ontology.

Scientific Ontology was not meant to be a grand theory that explained everything.

Rather, the opposite.

**It was a way for someone who thinks about all sorts of things to keep track of how far a claim may legitimately go.**

---

Physics is a demanding partner for this exercise.

There are definitions.

Equations.

Observations.

Existing theories.

Finding an idea interesting is not enough to get it through.

So I began taking objections apart, especially when they challenged me.

Is this ruled out by the definition?

Does it fail mathematically?

Has observation ruled it out?

Or is it simply something the current theory does not require?

Does the proposed structure fail to survive even when the conditions are changed?

Once I can distinguish these, something curious happens.

I can acknowledge a mistake without killing the question.

I cannot assert this as physics.

But I have learned why I cannot.

Then I can take that “why” home with me.

I want to bring back not only knowledge, but **the methods by which that knowledge was made**.

Why was that definition necessary?

Which observations made those conditions necessary?

What can I change before the theory breaks?

If I understand that much, then even when I carry the concept elsewhere, I at least know what I am breaking.

---

That is why I sometimes see a definition less as a wall than as an interface.

If I want to speak to physics, I use its definitions.

If I want to speak to economics, I check its definitions.

If I enter the domain of law, I return to its boundaries of responsibility.

There is no need to prohibit taking a concept into another field.

But the claims warranted by its original field do not automatically travel with it.

That is where the boundary lies.

A boundary is not a wall saying,

“Go no further.”

It is **a surface of contact that tells us what will cease to be warranted if we cross it**.

In this sense, we can cross boundaries.

But we must not forget that we have crossed one.

---

I still have some rather dubious questions.

Black holes are one of them.

I am not proposing an original physical theory of black holes here.

For me, however, black holes remain a personal problem at a boundary where questions about information, observation, time, inside and outside, and being able or unable to return come into view together.

I will probably keep getting into a little mischief.

In return, I will distinguish:

What has been established in physics.

What I have read into it.

What I have imagined on my own.

What has not been checked at all.

I will keep those apart.

Then there is room to play.

And when an objection lands a punch, there is a way back.

---

If I keep working this way, even the word “correct” begins to look different.

Is something correct because we have built walls around it so that nobody can break it?

I no longer think so.

A state made quiet by preventing objections from entering cannot, for that reason, be called correct.

Do not read the sources.

Do not listen to disagreement.

Forget that the claim had limited conditions.

An explanation made unbreakable that way has not become stronger.

Perhaps we have only closed the entrances through which it could be broken.

Science is not strong because it protects its current explanations.

We can return to observations.

Others can inspect the work.

Failures can be reported.

We can test again under different conditions.

The entrances through which the current theory might be broken are not completely sealed.

That is where the strength lies.

So this is the attitude I want to borrow from science:

**Follow the facts. Keep interpretation open for as long as possible.**

And:

**Be able to weaken a claim in order to keep a question alive.**

In English, I call this framework **Scientific Ontology**.

There was a time when I also called it 存在論科学 in Japanese—roughly, “ontological science.”

But as I kept asking how far a claim could legitimately go, the name itself became a question: was it stepping into the territory of science?

In Japanese, I now call it 存在境界論, *Sonzai Kyōkairon*—an inquiry into the boundaries of existence.

The English name still carries some of the pressure of the older name. Perhaps calling it “Scientific” is itself a kind of overstepping into science's domain. I am not claiming that the framework as a whole is an established science in the standard sense.

Still, there is a reason I retain the name.

**There are, at least, things I want to learn from science.**

Returning to observations.

Stating conditions explicitly.

Allowing others to examine what I say.

Not closing the entrances through which a difference capable of overturning the present explanation might arrive.

Not treating what remains unknown as if it were known.

“Scientific” names this orientation first, rather than certifying that the framework is already an established science.

Calling it that does not make it a science. What responsibilities the name commits me to, and which claims I am not yet in a position to make, remain open to questioning.

Perhaps that is what Scientific Ontology is for.

Not to harden the world into boundaries.

But to make boundaries visible, so that different things can come into contact without ceasing to be different.

Not to treat what we do not understand as understood.

Not to treat what is wrong as correct.

But not to take away the place of a question simply because it has no answer yet.

For me, Scientific Ontology is also

**a technique for keeping foolish questions without throwing them at other people as facts**.

That may be about right for a first explanation.

## 2. Do Not Let a Correct Answer End the Question
### Perhaps others are here to overturn what I call correct

I want correct answers.

I would rather have accurate information than misinformation.

I would rather know what has been checked than rely on an assumption.

So far, there is not much to hesitate over.

The difficulty comes next.

Once we have a correct answer, when may we stop thinking?

---

Ask an AI a question, and a well-organized answer may come back.

The conditions are set out.

The explanation is internally consistent.

Sources are supplied.

And still, something may remain.

Suppose, for example, that we ask,

“How can we make this work more efficient?”

and the AI produces a perfect improvement plan.

The answer may be correct.

But if the real difficulty was,

“Do we need to keep doing this work at all?”

then the problem has not ended.

The answer was not wrong.

The boundary of the question was too narrow.

**A well-answered question is not proof of a well-framed question.**

---

How far, then, must we investigate before we can say, “For now, this is correct”?

Within Scientific Ontology, I have worked out a rather demanding answer.

Simply finding no counterexample is not enough.

We may not have looked.

We may merely have shut out views we did not want to hear.

We may have run out of resources and stopped thinking.

So the state I now want to call “correct” needs at least three characteristics.

**We actually looked for something that could break it.**

**We continue that search within the time and methods currently available.**

And:

**If something new arrives tomorrow, we can open it again.**

This does not mean leaving judgment on hold forever.

When necessary, decide for now. Act. Close the matter.

But do not treat that closure as the end of collation with the world.

There is an entrance through which a difference can arrive.

There is a route back to the grounds of the judgment.

A judgment once closed can be reopened.

Scientific Ontology calls these Ingress, Traceback, and Reopen.

You do not need to remember the names.

In plain terms:

**We can hear something new. We can return to why we thought as we did. We can reconsider when necessary.**

That is what they mean.

This is not, however, a rule for turning “I am tired, so I will stop” into correctness. What matters is what we actually managed to examine, and how far. What counts as sufficient search remains an open question within this framework, too.

What I am describing here is an attitude toward correctness, not the full set of judgment conditions. A willingness to keep searching does not by itself establish that the present answer holds. The detailed account distinguishes the tenability of the judgment itself, actual search and its stopping conditions, and reopenability; readers can return to the [English commensuration of the canonical account of correctness](../../01_Sat_Truth/Correctness_Individuality_Absence_and_Cut.en.md), governed by its Japanese authoritative text.

---

Up to this point, it may sound like something a person could do alone.

In practice, that is difficult.

On our own, we can think only of objections that occur to us.

However earnestly we search, we may be circling within the same experiences, education, interests, fears, and past successes.

That is where others become necessary.

Other people are not valuable because they give me the same conclusions I already have.

They are valuable because they may have ways of breaking my conclusions that I could not find.

Looking at the same thing, they catch on a different point.

Looking at the same numbers, they recall a different history.

What does not even look like a problem to me may have been a problem for them all along.

Where I think I have “looked hard enough,” there may be an entrance only they can use.

In this sense, individuality is not merely taste or a style of expression.

Where did you enter the world?

What have you experienced?

What have you looked at repeatedly?

What have you overlooked?

Where do you direct your limited capacities?

Histories such as these change the shape of the world available to our search.

And searching requires resources.

Where do we obtain them? How do we allocate them, what do we use them for, and how do we handle them? Individuality and history enter here, too.

Individuality appears not only in what we think with the resources we are given, but also in how we make it possible to search for certain things at all.

---

That is why diversity has epistemic significance: it matters to what we can come to know.

This is not a claim that

“Everyone is different, and everyone is correct.”

Numbers do not turn a claim into truth.

Nor does individuality entitle an unsupported claim to be accepted.

Rather, the opposite.

**Different individualities can break one another's correctness.**

Someone else brings a counterexample that one person could not reach alone.

A premise that one person took for granted is not self-evident to another.

If something breaks there, we can go back.

We need not discard everything on the grounds that our former selves were foolish.

Perhaps that was as far as we could reach at that time, with those resources, along those paths of search.

Contact with another person expanded the world we could search.

The conditions under which correctness held therefore changed.

That is another way to read what happened.

---

This does not reduce other people to “devices for detecting my mistakes.”

They do not exist for my benefit.

What matters is recognizing that they, too, have entrances into the world that differ from mine.

I cannot fully possess the world as another person sees it.

Nor can that person fully possess me.

So we make contact.

Something breaks.

Something remains beyond our understanding.

Even then, we do not immediately make one of us the same as the other.

We do not rush from disagreement to enmity.

Sometimes we bring back a difference that cannot be integrated into a single answer.

There is another value of diversity here.

**Because others exist, I cannot close the world once and for all.**

I think that is a rather good thing.

---

The same applies to science.

Different instruments.

Different researchers.

Different conditions.

Replication.

Refutation.

Criticism.

These bring differences into the current explanation from outside.

For science to remain science, it needs entrances through which it can be challenged and broken.

Might the same be true of human correctness?

“I am correct”

and

“Therefore I no longer need to hear you”

are fundamentally different statements.

The word “correct” I now want to use is closer to this:

**With what I can presently do, I cannot break this any further. So I will proceed with it for now. But if you can break it, I have left an entrance open for that.**

Do not let a correct answer end the question.

That does not mean becoming indecisive.

Decide.

Act.

Close a matter.

Where necessary, do those things.

But:

**Do not confuse having closed a matter with there being nothing left in the world to consider.**

Within that small difference, there is room to keep thinking with others.

## 3. Introducing the Negentropy Ledger
### Not letting money alone decide our credits and debits

Imagine two companies making similar profits.

One has developed its people.

Repaired its equipment.

Left its business partners with technical knowledge.

Left its workers with skills they can take to their next job.

The other has worn people out.

Postponed replacing equipment.

Lost the people who know how to make repairs.

Pushed care burdens onto households.

Left waste in the environment.

In monetary accounts, both have revenue, costs, and profits.

Yet they do not seem to have done the same thing.

Could we write that difference into a ledger?

That was my first reason for thinking about a negentropy ledger.

---

Let me state something important at the outset.

This is not an argument against financial accounting.

Nor is it an attempt to turn physical entropy directly into economic value.

Nor is it a project to produce a grand total such as “one unit of health + one unit of forest + one unit of skill.”

The question I am asking is simpler.

**After that activity, what remains possible next?**

Someone worked.

They received a wage.

But can their body work again tomorrow?

Have their skills grown?

Is their capacity for judgment still there?

If they want to leave, can they?

A company made a profit.

But can it repair its equipment?

Can it pass on its technical knowledge?

Can it recover from an accident?

Does it have the reserves to move into another business?

For a country: will the next generation still be able to repair the roads?

Produce food?

Maintain medical care?

For the planet: can ecosystems continue into their next cycles?

We look not only at the present result, but at

**whether the possibilities for subsequent action have increased or diminished**.

I am thinking about a supplementary ledger for that purpose.

---

I borrow the word “negentropy” to think about this condition of still being able to do something next.

Here, however, negentropy is not a physical quantity.

Skills.

Knowledge.

Health.

Trust.

Equipment.

Ecosystems.

Repair capacity.

Reserves of judgment.

The possibility of leaving.

Room to try again.

Within Scientific Ontology, “negentropy” is a boundary term for considering such potential: a system's ability to continue maintaining itself, judging, repairing, and producing another state.

The negentropy ledger is therefore not a completed accounting standard.

No common unit of measurement has been established for it.

There is no method for adding everything into a single number, either.

We are not yet at that stage.

**First, open accounts for what we have not been seeing.**

---

What matters is keeping separate ledgers for different boundaries.

**Do not look at just one ledger.**

My first question is less about the numbers themselves than about which ledger is being allowed to end the discussion.

One ledger can balance perfectly while something is diminishing within another boundary. A company's profit, a worker's body, a household's time, a supplier's repair capacity, and an ecosystem's capacity to regenerate do not all fit into the same account.

So we should not rush to build a single grand ledger in which everything can be offset against everything else.

**Keep the ledgers separate, and place them side by side.**

Look at the same event from several boundaries at once.

Is the surplus within one boundary being made out of a deficit within another? Has a cost that disappeared here merely been transferred into someone else's body or future?

**Before adding things up, look at them separately. Before offsetting them, trace where a burden has moved from and where it has gone.**

A company's surplus is not necessarily its employees' surplus.

A company reduces its workforce and increases its profit.

Its monetary accounts show a surplus.

But if the remaining workers are exhausted, there is a deficit in their bodily ledger.

If a burden moves into the household, a deficit has moved there.

If a skilled worker leaves and the route to repair disappears, a deficit remains in the ledger of future capacity as well.

The reverse can also happen.

Training reduces this period's profit.

Spare equipment is retained.

More leave is provided.

In terms of short-term efficiency alone, the result may look bad.

But if readiness for accidents, skills, reserves of judgment, and the possibility of leaving have increased, then assets have grown in another ledger.

**Being unused is not the same as being wasted.**

Sometimes the room we leave unused is itself a future option.

---

Exchange begins to look different, too.

Exchange need not be zero-sum.

Something of little value to me may be of great value to you.

Knowledge can sometimes be passed on without disappearing from the person who gives it.

Cooperation can produce something that neither person could have produced alone.

Value can grow.

But:

**An increase in value does not entitle us to erase the history of its effects.**

What made its creation possible?

Where did the resources come from?

Where did fatigue remain?

What was lost?

Which burden moved across another boundary?

Can the damage be repaired later?

Trace that far.

I put it this way:

**Value can grow. Do not let the consequences go unaccounted for.**

“Accounting for the consequences” does not mean that the universe contains a grand conservation law of value.

What I want to preserve is not a total quantity of value, but

**the provenance of effects, and the paths by which we can return to examine them**.

---

The ledger does not decide the answer.

How much may we spend to develop skills?

What should we do when environmental needs and employment conflict?

What happens when an individual's ability to leave conflicts with an organization's continuity?

A ledger cannot decide these things by itself.

We need ethics.

Law.

Politics.

Economics.

Science.

And the wishes of the person concerned.

The negentropy ledger is not a judge.

It is **a ledger that keeps evidence from falling off the judge's desk**.

---

At this stage, I do not think its incompleteness is only a weakness.

The ledger could become an interface with many different fields.

Connected to accounting, it makes visible the boundary between recognized assets and value that is not recognized as an asset.

Connected to labor, it brings into view the body, skills, and possibilities of exit that remain outside the wage.

Connected to environmental questions, it reveals the difference in timescale between present profit and regenerative capacity.

Connected to organizational thinking, it reveals redundancy, repair capacity, and the transmission of knowledge.

Connected to ethics, it asks whose surplus was made out of whose deficit.

Connected to thinking about peace, it asks whether prosperity within one boundary is being built by accumulating burdens, humiliation, or future reasons for retaliation within another.

Beyond this point, much remains unfinished.

What counts as a stock?

What counts as a flow?

How do we prevent double counting?

How far may different scales be compared?

Who makes the evaluation?

How should future possibilities be entered in the books?

Many spaces remain blank.

Even so, the first question is simple.

**After this work, this exchange, this institution, this year—what remains possible next?**

If something increased, look for its source.

If something decreased, look for where it went.

If the account does not balance within one boundary, open the inquiry beyond it.

Only then can we write seemingly strange things into a ledger:

“We made a profit. Yet we became poorer.”

“It cost more. Yet we became stronger.”

“We did nothing. Yet we protected our reserves.”

This is not a finished theory.

That is precisely why I want to connect it to different places and see where it breaks.

## 4. Toward a Peace That Does Not Take Boundary Violation for Granted
### Without taking away the right to strike back, reduce the situations in which striking back is the only way to recover a boundary

We know that violating a boundary can bring a response against us.

Violate a national border, and a counterattack may follow.

Take someone else's property, and they resist.

Touch someone's body without permission, and they refuse.

Use power beyond the agreed scope, and they protest.

A company pushes burdens onto a community, and resistance emerges somewhere.

Human beings have known this for a long time.

So we made laws.

Armies.

Police forces.

Contracts.

We thought about deterrence.

About limits to retaliation.

About how far a counter-response may go.

In other words, we have spent a very long time thinking about

**managing boundary violations and the responses they provoke**.

Yet war has not disappeared.

Nor has violence.

Nor exploitation.

Perhaps it is time to move the question one step back.

**Instead of asking only how to strike back, why do we begin by treating boundary violation as a normal way to make contact?**

---

What I mean here by boundary violation is not simply touching or crossing a boundary.

To live is to make contact.

We eat.

Borrow.

Sell.

Work.

Teach.

Learn.

Love.

Criticize.

Help.

Receive resources from nature.

We cannot live without others.

So peace cannot mean nothing more than keeping every boundary inviolable.

Contact is not the problem.

The problem is **deciding the terms of contact on our side alone, as though the other side had no boundary of its own**.

Take.

Enter.

Decide.

Use.

Define.

Only when something comes back do we notice:

“They have struck back.”

I want to change that sequence itself.

**What can pass is not necessarily what should be let through.**

The question is not only whether a route exists. What passes, who lets it through, and under what conditions? How is it handled on the other side, and how is it transformed before it comes back?

Do we let something through simply because we can? Or do we judge what to let through, while leaving a path by which its return can lead us to reconsider if our judgment was wrong?

Creating a route alone does not settle that difference.

---

Of course, we must not simply prohibit striking back.

This matters.

If, in the name of peace, we say only,

“You must not hit back,”

we give an overwhelming advantage to whoever can land the first blow.

Only the center may impose sanctions.

Only the state has force.

Only the company decides.

Only the experts define.

Only the administrator has access.

Demanding that those at the margins “be peaceful” under those conditions is not peace.

Quiet obtained by stripping away the right to strike back, to protest, to leave, or to seek restoration may be nothing more than silence.

What I want to protect here is room to resist a violation, to protest, to defend oneself, and to seek restoration. This does not justify unlimited retaliation.

So I am not trying to create

**a world in which people cannot strike back**.

What I want is

**a world with as few situations as possible in which people must strike back to recover their boundaries**.

Those are different things.

---

When a boundary is trampled, anger appears.

Protest appears.

Refusal appears.

These are also forms of return: something coming back to tell us that a boundary exists.

We must not erase that return.

But neither should we keep converting it directly into retaliation.

We were hurt, so we hurt back.

We were struck back at, so we respond again.

As that history accumulates, the original boundary violation may fade, leaving only:

“The other side is the enemy.”

This is where the return needs to be transformed.

If we can respond in words, respond in words.

Ask for an explanation.

Identify responsibility.

Provide compensation.

Restore what can be restored.

Renegotiate.

Refer the matter to law.

Bring in a third party.

Close a matter for a time.

Change the conditions, and open it again.

Do not abolish the return.

**Before it flares into retaliation, create more routes by which a boundary can be restored.**

If peace can be designed, perhaps this is where it begins.

---

Reading Kant's *Toward Perpetual Peace* through Scientific Ontology, one point matters to me.

A peace settlement alone does not make peace.

If the settlement contains reasons that can be used to justify the next war, war has merely been sent into the future.

Even during hostilities, do not burn away the possibility of speaking again.

In the vocabulary of Scientific Ontology:

**Do not build tomorrow's reasons for retaliation into today's victory.**

The victor leaves the defeated in complete humiliation.

Builds institutions in which they cannot protest.

Takes resources until recovery is impossible.

Reserves the authority to interpret everything for itself.

After all that, declaring “Now there is peace” does not make the return disappear.

It has only moved out of sight.

One day it may come back in another form.

---

That is why peace needs boundary diplomacy.

Diplomacy does not mean becoming friends.

Nor does it mean coming to think alike.

It means keeping routes for connection open even when disagreement remains.

How far may we enter?

What will we share?

What will we not share?

Under what conditions do we stop?

Who takes responsibility?

Where can an objection be sent back?

Under what conditions can a closed route be opened again?

Build that interface.

In computing, entering someone else's system with administrator privileges and without permission is not what we ordinarily call communication.

There are protocols.

Permissions.

Ports.

Procedures for failure.

Might boundaries in human society be less different than we suppose?

There is no need to open everything.

Some places must be closed for safety.

But:

**Closing a route is different from treating the other side as nonexistent.**

**Not connecting is different from destroying even the possibility of connection.**

---

Here I return to science.

Science makes boundaries, too.

Physics has its definitions.

Biology has its methods.

Psychology has its measurements.

Those boundaries let us check what is being claimed and on what grounds.

But surely the boundaries of science are not supposed to be fortress walls saying,

“Do not think about anything outside.”

At least, what I want to learn from science is the opposite.

This much has been measured.

This much has been reproduced.

Beyond here, we do not yet know.

It held under these conditions.

Under different conditions, we must test again.

Science is not strong because it defines the world once and fixes it in place.

**It is strong because another observation can reopen the current explanation.**

Boundaries are not there for permanent blockade.

They make clear what holds, and how far it holds, so that different things can make contact without destroying one another.

Could social boundaries move in the same direction?

---

The world has come a long way in understanding this much:

“Boundaries will be violated. Be ready to strike back.”

From that came deterrence.

Balances of power.

Defense.

These are necessary.

There is no need to discard them.

But we must not mistake them for peace in its completed form.

To move further, we need to

**reduce the arrangements in which achieving our purposes requires trampling someone else's boundary**.

We cannot be safe unless another country submits.

We cannot make a profit unless we wear away our employees' lives.

We cannot be prosperous unless we take more than nature can regenerate.

We cannot reach agreement unless the other side's words disappear.

Gradually replace such arrangements with different ones.

This is not a matter of goodwill.

It is a matter of design.

Permission.

Scope.

Time limits.

Objection.

Exit.

Compensation.

Return paths.

Renegotiation.

Reconnection.

Put these in place first.

Instead of violating a boundary and then calculating the retaliation:

**Before making contact, establish how that contact will take place.**

---

Even then, boundaries will be violated.

There is malice.

Misunderstanding.

Accident.

There are things that only force can stop.

So do not discard defense.

Do not discard the right to strike back.

But do not accept the need for them as a natural condition of human society beyond the possibility of change.

I do not think peace means that conflict never occurs.

Nor that everyone shares the same values.

Nor that all boundaries disappear.

Rather:

**Different things continue to exist as different; they can make contact when needed and can refuse it; when something breaks, they can send a return and bring responsibility back to where it belongs—and still be able to connect again in the future.**

Maintaining such a condition is much harder.

That is why peace needs practical techniques.

Institutions.

Ethics.

Science.

Diplomacy.

And the simple recognition that the other side has boundaries, too.

I do not want to harden the world into boundaries.

I want to end a world in which we trample one another because those boundaries remain unseen.

**Do not abolish boundaries. Stop treating their violation as an ordinary form of communication.**

Perhaps that is where Scientific Ontology's account of peace should begin.
