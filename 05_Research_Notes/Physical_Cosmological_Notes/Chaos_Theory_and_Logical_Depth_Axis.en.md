> Language: English commensurated rendering

## 0. Position

This note is a research-note draft for rereading chaos theory from the perspective of Scientific Ontology.

It does not replace chaos theory, dynamical systems theory, physics, or empirical science.

What this note does is examine the relation between the unpredictability treated by chronological nonlinear determinism and the concepts of internal count-time, intrinsic time, the logical-depth axis, residual preservation, and history-field correspondence in Scientific Ontology.

Chaos theory shows that even a system governed by deterministic rules may lose long-term predictability through nonlinearity and sensitive dependence on initial conditions.

However, chaos is not simple disorder.

Within chaotic behavior, deterministic structures such as strange attractors, fractal structures, and periodic windows may appear.

Accordingly, chaos theory can be read not as a simple opposition between order and disorder, but as a theory that treats deterministic rules, nonlinear amplification, unpredictability, and the reappearance of structure at the same time.

Scientific Ontology does not deny chaos theory.

Rather, it respects the unpredictability treated by chronological nonlinear determinism and then asks a different question.

That question is as follows.

Which differences that appear contingent in chronological sequence become meaningful differences in which history-fields?

Which differences are preserved as residuals?

Which residuals sink into the logical-depth axis?

Which unclosed structures alter later recognition, judgment, and meaning-generation?

The center of this note is not to extend chaos theory itself.

It is to use the compatibility between deterministic structure and unpredictability shown by chaos theory as an entrance into the problem of historical difference, intrinsic time, and the logical-depth axis, which cannot be fully handled by external chronological sequence alone.

## 1. Chaos Theory as Nonlinear Determinism

In one sentence, chaos theory is a theory that treats how long-term prediction of behavior becomes difficult even in deterministically composed systems because of nonlinearity.

However, not every nonlinear system becomes chaotic.

Nor does unpredictability mean that no rule exists.

In chaos, there are local rules.

There are update rules.

The next state is derived from the initial conditions.

Yet because slight differences are amplified through temporal development, long-term prediction breaks down.

In this sense, chaos theory shows that determinism and predictability are not identical.

Being deterministic does not guarantee being predictable.

This point is important for Scientific Ontology.

The reason is that similar phenomena occur in human judgment, institutions, meaning-generation, creation, research, and relationships.

On an external chronological sequence, an event may appear contingent.

However, when history, premises, unresolved issues, residuals, semantic pressure, and unsettled responsibility are included, that event may be strongly constrained.

Accordingly, within Scientific Ontology, chaos theory becomes an entrance into the following question.

Is what appears contingent really contingent?

Or does it appear contingent because it is being viewed only through external chronological sequence?

## 2. What Chaos Theory Leaves Open

Chaos theory mainly treats state changes along chronological sequence.

There is a state at a certain time.

There is an update rule.

There is a next state.

Differences are amplified nonlinearly.

Long-term prediction becomes difficult.

However, what Scientific Ontology asks is not only state change itself.

The problem is in which history-field a certain difference becomes a meaningful difference.

The same difference may be processed as noise in one system.

In another system, it may be preserved as a residual.

In still another system, it may sink as history that changes later judgment.

Therefore, in Scientific Ontology, what matters is not only the amplification of difference.

Which differences become meaningful?

Which differences are preserved as residuals?

Which differences settle as residues?

Which differences alter later recognition, judgment, and meaning-generation?

Here, residual and residue are provisionally distinguished.

A residual is an unclosed difference that cannot be fully closed through operational correspondence, classification, or meaning-formation, and is preserved for future questioning or reconstruction.

A residue is a historical remainder that continues to sediment in a field and act as debt, discomfort, or distortion even after it has been treated as processed.

Neither is mere noise.

They may alter later reception, judgment, and meaning-generation.

This question may not be sufficiently handled by chronological nonlinear determinism alone.

The reason is that how a difference is amplified along chronological sequence and in which history-field that difference becomes meaningful, and at what depth it is preserved, are not the same problem.

## 3. Three Layers of Time

When connecting chaos theory to Scientific Ontology, time should not be treated as a single flat layer.

This note distinguishes at least the following three layers.

1. Internal count-time
2. Intrinsic time
3. Logical-depth axis

These three are connected, but they are not identical.

### 3.1 Internal Count-Time

Internal count-time is an internal counter corresponding to external time.

It is time used to synchronize with the external world, such as clocks, dates, deadlines, schedules, age, and working hours.

Internal count-time has strong reality externally.

A meeting starts at a certain time.

A deadline is set for a certain date.

A train arrives at a certain minute.

Working time is counted in hours.

Society, institutions, contracts, records, labor, transportation, and schedules strongly depend on this time.

Internally, however, internal count-time does not necessarily have strong reality.

This is because it does not directly indicate what one is committed to. It indicates how one is synchronized with the timetable of the external world.

It remains in the schedule.

It remains in the record.

However, that time does not necessarily live densely inside one’s history-field.

Therefore, internal count-time is the time of operational correspondence.

It is necessary.

However, by itself, it cannot explain the phenomenon in which an event that is over externally has not yet ended internally.

### 3.2 Intrinsic Time

Intrinsic time is the order of updating within a history-field.

It is not external clock time itself.

It is an internal order indicating what is processed first, what is processed later, and what remains unprocessed while continuing to have present force within a history-field.

Intrinsic time is the time of commitment.

What was one involved in?

What did one take on?

What has not yet closed?

Which history still acts on current judgment?

Intrinsic time treats these questions.

Even if only one hour has passed by the external clock, that hour may become extremely dense under deep concentration or strong decision.

Conversely, even if ten years have passed by the external clock, an unprocessed event may still act as present within intrinsic time.

In this sense, intrinsic time does not treat external timestamp. It treats the updating order inside a history-field and the present force of unprocessed history.

### 3.3 Logical-Depth Axis

The logical-depth axis is not time itself.

Rather, it is an axis for reading unclosed structures that remain within intrinsic time according to the depth at which they are preserved.

Intrinsic time treats what is updated in what order inside a history-field.

The logical-depth axis treats at what depth unresolved issues, contradictions, branches, residuals, residues, and semantic pressure are preserved and reconstructed.

These two are connected, but they are not identical.

Intrinsic time concerns updating order.

The logical-depth axis concerns the preservation depth of unclosed structures.

Therefore, when chaos theory is connected to Scientific Ontology, it is not enough simply to replace external time with intrinsic time.

The problem is which nonlinear differences sink into which history-field at which depth, and as which demand for re-collation they return.

### 3.4 External Time as a Synchronization Axis

In the reading of Scientific Ontology, external time can be considered an external projection of intrinsic time.

However, this does not mean that the intrinsic time of one subject directly becomes external time.

Multiple subjects have their own intrinsic times and carry their own commitments while synchronizing with the same external world.

As a composite projection of these conditions, time in the external world appears to “flow.”

External time is a high-entropy synchronization axis used to coordinate commitments, institutions, schedules, records, observations, responsibilities, and communications among multiple subjects.

On the other hand, when one is deeply concentrated conceptually, one may forget external time.

This may be because commitment on low-entropy intrinsic time becomes more operative than high-entropy count-time for synchronizing with the external world.

This hypothesis is not presented here as a physical theory.

It is only a working hypothesis inside Scientific Ontology for distinguishing external time, internal count-time, intrinsic time, and the logical-depth axis.

A similar analogy may also be possible for space.

However, this note does not enter into a theory of space.

## 4. Apparent Contingency and Structural Constraint

Chaos theory is important for Scientific Ontology because it shows that deterministic rules and unpredictability can coexist.

Along chronological sequence, an event may appear contingent.

One happened to hear those words.

One happened to be in that place.

One happened to make that judgment.

One happened to make that choice.

However, from the standpoint of intrinsic time, that event is not necessarily contingent.

There may already be accumulated commitments, unprocessed residuals, unclosed meanings, past judgments, and discomforts that have not yet been processed.

Further, from the standpoint of the logical-depth axis, the event becomes a question of which unclosed structure reacted at which depth.

In this sense, something appears contingent because the event is being seen only through external chronological sequence.

From the logical-depth axis, the event appears as the result of a reaction by an unclosed structure preserved in a history-field.

The structural constraint described here is not fatalism.

It does not mean that everything has been decided from the beginning.

It also does not mean that any action will lead to the same result.

Nor does it mean that physical determinism is directly transferred to human meaning-generation or institutional history.

Rather, it means the following.

If certain residuals, premises, contradictions, and commitments are preserved within a history-field, there is often a strong structural reason why a certain event returns with a particular meaning.

In external time, it looks contingent.

In intrinsic time, what had not yet been processed reacts.

On the logical-depth axis, one can see which depth of unclosed structure the event has touched.

Accordingly, this note preserves the following thesis.

> What appears contingent may be the form something takes when it is viewed only through external chronological sequence.  
> From the logical-depth axis, it may appear as the result of a reaction by an unclosed structure preserved in a history-field, and it is often strongly constrained structurally.

This thesis is not a standard thesis of chaos theory.

It is a research hypothesis by which Scientific Ontology uses the intuition of chaos theory as an entrance into history-fields, intrinsic time, and the logical-depth axis.

## 5. Logical-Depth Correspondence, Not Physical Superluminality

The logical-depth axis generates correspondences that cannot be closed by external spacetime order alone.

A past event may be past in external time, yet still act as present within intrinsic time.

A distant event may reconnect to the same field of judgment not through physical contact, but through meaning, institution, memory, responsibility, and informational residuals.

In this sense, the logical-depth axis relativizes the order of external spacetime.

However, this is not a claim that it transcends spacetime in the physical sense.

This note does not claim superluminal communication.

It does not claim a violation of causality.

It does not claim identity with entanglement in standard physics.

It does not propose a physical theory that makes external spacetime meaningless.

What is being described here is historical correspondence that is not reducible to external spacetime order.

History-fields may have correspondences that are difficult to describe by external spacetime order alone, not through physical entanglement, but through residuals, meaning, responsibility, memory, institutions, and informational pressure.

This correspondence is not physical nonlocality.

It is logical-depth correspondence.

Therefore, this note does not treat the logical-depth axis as physical transcendence.

It treats the logical-depth axis as an axis for reading at what depth unclosed structures are preserved within history-fields and how they return to later fields of judgment.

## 6. History-Gravity Domain

The logical-depth axis relativizes the order of external spacetime.

However, this does not mean complete freedom.

Unclosed structures do not exist alone in a vacuum.

They are preserved within some history-field.

When this history-field stabilizes internal communication and makes external collation easier to cut off, this note calls it a history-gravity domain.

However, a history-gravity domain does not simply mean “a force by which past history pulls on the present.”

A history-gravity domain is an autonomous communicative region in which the area around a certain history is cut off by Null, making external communication easier to close.

Here, Null does not mean simple empty space.

It is a boundary where communication is cut off, the return path for collation is lost, and what lies beyond is processed as “not our world.”

Inside a history-gravity domain, certain premises, values, memories, roles, responsibilities, and uses of language are shared.

For that reason, communication works internally.

However, from the outside, those premises themselves are difficult to see.

Questions from outside are often processed as “irrelevant,” “not understanding,” or “nonexistent.”

At this point, a history-gravity domain begins to acquire independence as a belief system.

It is not a mere assumption.

History, institution, memory, language, role, responsibility, fear, successful experience, and failed experience become connected to one another and stabilize internal communication.

The higher that stability becomes, the more difficult external collation becomes.

The logical-depth axis is also an axis for reading which unclosed structures are preserved at which depths inside this history-gravity domain.

In other words, the problem is not merely that “the past pulls on the present.”

The problem is the following.

Inside which historical domain does something communicate, fail to communicate, get cut off as Null, and form a belief system that refuses external collation?

The concept of the history-gravity domain is needed in order to read that structure.

For this reason, the logical-depth axis and the history-gravity domain are distinguished.

The logical-depth axis reads the depth of unclosed structures.

The history-gravity domain reads the communicative region in which those unclosed structures are preserved.

The fact that residuals return is not itself a history-gravity domain.

It is a phenomenon in which an unclosed structure demands re-collation inside a history-gravity domain.

## 7. Research Hypothesis

The hypothesis of this note can be organized as follows.

Chaos theory shows the structure by which chronological nonlinear determinism loses long-term predictability.

On the other hand, Scientific Ontology does not ask only about unpredictability along chronological sequence.

Scientific Ontology asks which differences sink as history, which residuals are preserved on the logical-depth axis, and which unclosed structures alter later recognition, judgment, and meaning-generation.

Therefore, chronological nonlinear determinism alone may be insufficient to describe the problem of history-fields that includes the logical-depth axis.

The problem of history-fields here is not physical determinism.

It is structural constraint in the following sense: within a history-field, past contacts, unprocessed residuals, institutional disconnections, semantic pressure, and unsettled responsibility constrain later reception, judgment, and meaning-generation.

This note organizes that structural constraint through the following three layers.

1. Internal count-time treats synchronization with the external world.
2. Intrinsic time treats updating order and commitment inside a history-field.
3. The logical-depth axis treats the preservation depth of unclosed structures.

Further, the history-gravity domain treats the communicative region in which those unclosed structures are preserved.

With this organization, it may become possible to describe cases in which events that appear contingent along chronological sequence are strongly constrained structurally inside a history-field.

## 8. Scope Boundary

This note does not deny chaos theory.

This note does not replace chaos theory with Scientific Ontology.

This note does not make new empirical claims about entanglement, gravity, spacetime, or causality in standard physics.

This note does not propose a physical theory that makes external spacetime meaningless.

This note does not replace psychology, sociology, institutional theory, physics, or dynamical systems theory.

What this note does is to respect chaos theory as a theory of chronological nonlinearity and then place, outside or behind it, the problems of internal count-time, intrinsic time, the logical-depth axis, the history-gravity domain, and residual preservation.

This research note preserves the following questions.

How does a nonlinear difference along chronological sequence shift into historical difference?

Under what conditions is a historical difference preserved as a residual?

Under what conditions does a residual become a residue?

How do residuals and residues return to later fields of judgment?

How far can the logical-depth axis describe this mode of return?

How far can the history-gravity domain describe the communicative region in which that mode of return becomes possible?

## 9. Provisional Conclusion

Chaos theory shows that deterministic rules and unpredictability can coexist.

Scientific Ontology asks, from that point, how differences that appear unpredictable become historical, at what depth they are preserved, and how they return to fields of meaning, responsibility, and judgment.

Therefore, the provisional conclusion of this note is as follows.

Chaos theory is an excellent theory of nonlinear determinism in external chronological sequence.

However, the problem of history-fields that includes the logical-depth axis is not closed by unpredictability along chronological sequence alone.

This note does not overcome chaos theory.

Rather, it respects chaos theory as a theory of nonlinear difference-amplification in external chronological sequence and then places outside it the questions of historical difference, residual preservation, the logical-depth axis, and the history-gravity domain.

The minimum conclusion of this note is as follows.

> Internal count-time treats synchronization with the external world.  
> Intrinsic time treats updating order and commitment inside a history-field.  
> The logical-depth axis treats the preservation depth of unclosed structures.  
> The history-gravity domain treats the communicative region in which those unclosed structures are preserved.

By distinguishing these four, the problem of historical difference that cannot be fully handled by chronological nonlinearity can be preserved as a research note in Scientific Ontology.

When viewed chronologically, something may appear contingent.

However, when viewed from the history-field, intrinsic time, and the logical-depth axis, it may be strongly constrained structurally.

This view is not chaos theory itself.

Yet the “coexistence of deterministic structure and unpredictability” shown by chaos theory becomes an important entrance, within Scientific Ontology, for thinking about historical structures that cannot be seen through external chronological sequence alone.
