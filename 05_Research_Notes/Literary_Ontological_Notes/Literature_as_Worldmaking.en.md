# Literature as Worldmaking
## The Operational Structure of Worlds through Truth, Beauty, Goodness, and Null

> Status: Public Research Note / Living Draft
> Layer: Research Note
> Domain: Literary Ontology / Worldmaking
> Language: English commensurated rendering
> Japanese authoritative source: [`Literature_as_Worldmaking.ja.md`](./Literature_as_Worldmaking.ja.md)
> Claim strength: S2–S3
> Scope: This document describes how literary works make and present the conditions under which a world can arise, and how they make observable the consequences of causality, communication, history, responsibility, and meaning within that world. It does not reduce literary value in general to a single criterion, nor does it provide a scoring system for judging individual works.

---

## 0. Positioning

Scientific Ontology begins from the recognition that worlds are made.

Naming, classification, institutions, contracts, diagnosis, scientific models, and interfaces do not merely describe an already existing world from a neutral position. They alter what can be seen, what can be done, who is protected, which histories remain, and what returns as a downstream condition.

Literature is also a form of worldmaking.

Literary worldmaking, however, is not identical to creating large quantities of fictional geography, history, species, magical systems, or other setting elements. A short poem, an autobiographical novel, or documentary literature close to recorded reality can also remake what may appear, which differences become significant, which events can reach which other events, whose pain becomes observable, and what remains unspoken.

What a work presents is not an inventory of things.

It presents a way of receiving a world and the conditions under which that world operates.

This note begins from the following proposition:

> Literature is a forum in which worldmaking is presented.

Here, "forum" does not mean only an exhibition of finished products or a competition of skill. It means a site in which a world made by an author is opened to the observation of others, allowing what occurs under that world's conditions to become collatable among author, work, and reader.

This note therefore does not close literature into any single category such as imitation of reality, authorial self-expression, information transmission, entertainment, or moral instruction. It treats literature as an activity that locally rearranges the conditions under which a world can arise and passes the consequences of that arrangement into observation.

---

## 1. What Does Literature Make?

### 1.1 What Is Made Is Not Only "Setting"

When discussing fictional worlds, the most visible elements are often characters, places, periods, institutions, technologies, and customs. These are only the parts of worldmaking that are easiest to state explicitly.

What literature makes includes at least:

- what can appear as existent;
- what can count as an event;
- which differences acquire meaning;
- whose observation enters the description of the world;
- what becomes a cause and what returns as an effect;
- how far actions, emotions, memories, and responsibilities can reach;
- what remains as history and what is forgotten;
- what is left unsaid and what remains unsayable; and
- where a severed connection may become reconnectable.

In this sense, a work that adopts physical laws almost identical to those of ordinary reality is still making a world. What is made need not be an object or law absent from reality. The arrangement of already existing elements—what is brought near or pushed away, illuminated or backgrounded—is itself worldmaking.

### 1.2 Worldmaking Is Not Ownership of a World

An author may appear free to determine everything written into a work. Once a world within the work begins to operate, however, the initially established conditions generate downstream conditions.

If a character is given a history, it becomes difficult to treat that character later as a convenient historyless component. If a society is given an institution, consequences arise concerning whom that institution protects and onto whom it shifts burdens. If death is established as irreversible and the dead are later restored too casually, the weight of the choices and losses of those who lived under that condition also changes.

The ability to make a world is not the same as the right to handle the made world in any manner whatsoever.

Worldmaking encounters resistance returned by the conditions that have been made. That resistance may return from collation with reality outside the work, from internal consistency, from the histories of characters, or from meaning received by readers.

Worldmaking is therefore not ownership, but the setting of conditions and the operation of a world in which the consequences of those conditions must be borne.

### 1.3 Novels Are Not the Only Forms That Make Worlds

The term worldmaking is easily associated with novels containing expansive fictional universes. The size of the made world, however, is not identical to the depth of worldmaking.

A single line of poetry may bring what had remained in the background into the foreground and leave an irreversible difference in the reader's perception. First-person narration may present the same sequence of facts as a different arrangement of pain and responsibility. A work structured around silence may leave the pressure of what was not spoken more strongly than an explicit account could.

A literary world does not arise from breadth alone.

It arises through what comes into contact, what remains as history, and how that history changes what can be received next.

## 2. Literature as a Forum for Presenting Worldmaking

### 2.1 What Is Presented Is Not an Answer, but a World

A literary work differs from an explanatory text organized around propositions. Even when a work contains a claim, extracting that claim alone may fail to recover what the work does.

A work can do more than state that an idea is correct. It can present a world in which that idea has been adopted and show how people live there, what is damaged, what forms of beauty appear, and what can no longer return.

What the reader receives is not merely the author's conclusion.

The reader receives a world containing the arrangement that produces the conclusion, the residuals that fall outside it, and consequences that even the author could not fully close.

For this reason, a work can be read beyond the author's intention. This is not only a matter of authorial authority being lost. It means that the made world has begun to produce downstream conditions that cannot be reduced to the author's explanation.

### 2.2 Author, Work, and Reader

A forum for presenting worldmaking contains at least three positions.

| Position | Primary function |
|---|---|
| Author | selects conditions, arranges them, and opens or closes narrative ports |
| Work | retains established conditions and histories, generating consequences beyond authorial intention |
| Reader | temporarily inhabits the world, receives differences, and collates them with their own history |

A reader is not merely a judge standing outside the work and assigning a score. While reading, the reader is a temporary inhabitant who experiences what can be seen and unseen in that world and the speed at which meaning returns.

At the same time, the reader is also a tester of the world.

A causal relation that appeared closed within the work may first break down when it contacts the reader's history. Conversely, an experience that could not be connected in ordinary reality may begin to form a Meaning Loop through the work.

This does not mean that free interpretation by the reader nullifies every condition retained by the work. Reading arises between generative activity from the reader's history and resistance retained by the work.

### 2.3 The Forum Does Not Close

A work may become physically fixed when writing is completed. The worldmaking that occurs through the work does not thereby end.

As historical conditions change, institutions that once remained in the background may come to the foreground. When a work is commensurated into another language, differences compressed into a single expression in the original language may unfold. When readers with different histories enter the work, different Return Paths may open.

A literary work is a pair consisting of fixed inscription and repeatedly renewed contact.

The "forum" is therefore not a one-time act of publication. Each reading creates contact between the made world and another history-field, and part of the world's consequences becomes observable again.

## 3. Phase Differences among Truth, Beauty, and Goodness

Literature does not establish Truth, Beauty, and Goodness in the same manner.

This note distinguishes them by treating Truth as capable of concerning proto-structure or pre-structure, while Beauty and Goodness emerge after structure and observation have arisen. This does not divide them into completely separate domains. The same event may concern Truth as fundamental arrangement, Beauty as the perceptible emergence of difference, and Goodness as the handling of history.

### 3.1 Truth — What Resists Narration

Truth does not refer only to whether an already established proposition is correct.

Truth may resist structure as an inconsistency that remains even when a narrator hides it, an institution renames it, or an observer would prefer it to disappear.

In this sense, Truth can concern not only the interior of an already structured world, but also what that structure depends on, what it cuts off, and what it leaves out in order to arise.

That Truth may concern proto-structure or pre-structure does not mean that these can be directly grasped, possessed, or exhaustively explained.

In literature, Truth often appears not as an answer but as a tear that remains when the work attempts to close its world: an action that cannot be recovered by a character's explanation, pain that does not disappear even though the plot has resolved, or history pushed outside narration that nevertheless reaches the reader.

Fiction does not necessarily stand in opposition to this Truth.

Even without reproducing the factual sequence of reality, fiction may rearrange elements so that structural truth backgrounded in ordinary reality becomes visible. Literary fiction can therefore be an operation that moves away from factual sequence while also approaching Truth by another route.

### 3.2 Beauty — Difference Becoming Perceptible Form

Beauty arises on the side of observation after structure.

Here, Beauty does not mean only pleasing appearance or harmony. It includes difference, relation, movement, tension, and rupture acquiring a form that an observer can receive.

Even when the underlying structure is the same, the observed form changes depending on sequence, distance, repetition, and cutoff. Rhythm, point of view, metaphor, blank space, and scene arrangement are not decorative additions to information. They are acts of making that determine how differences in a world become perceptible.

Beauty can make Truth easier to see, and it can conceal it.

An excessively well-arranged narrative can make unresolved histories appear closed. Conversely, forms that retain ugliness, imbalance, incompletion, or silence can sometimes transmit ruptures in the world more precisely.

Beauty is therefore not packaging for Truth.

It is an independent mode of making concerned with the form and movement through which a world arises on the side of observation.

### 3.3 Goodness — How Established History Is Handled

Goodness also arises after structure.

Once boundaries arise, subjects become differentiated, contact occurs, and history remains, questions emerge about what is protected, what is returned, who bears responsibility, and where an operation should stop.

Goodness in literature does not mean placing morally good characters into the story or presenting a correct lesson.

It concerns how a work handles the harm and loss it has generated. Does it consume characters merely as material for a conclusion? Does redemption trample over history? Does it treat something irreparable as though it had been returned or repaired? How does it arrange the right to remain silent and the right to demand a response?

These are questions of communication protocol and responsibility processing within the made world.

Goodness does not mean keeping a world undamaged. It is an operational mode concerned with preserving histories produced by contact, not monopolizing Return Paths, not treating the irreparable as repaired, and leaving conditions for future action.

### 3.4 Overlap among the Three Modes

Truth, Beauty, and Goodness are not separate components of a work.

Suppose a character dies.

- The world structure that made the death possible and the consequences that cannot be erased by narrative convenience concern Truth.
- The distance, sequence, rhythm, and imagery through which the death becomes perceptible concern Beauty.
- Who bears the history of the dead, who forgets, what is returned, and what remains unreturned concern Goodness.

Distinguishing these modes helps prevent a work from being reduced to any one of "a correct claim," "beautiful expression," or "a good lesson."

## 4. Null — When Connection Is Lost within a World

### 4.1 Canonical Definition

This note uses `Null / Void` according to the following canonical definition:

> A state in which historical connection is severed and a Meaning Loop cannot form or persist.

Null is not identical to simple nonexistence.

A person, event, or memory being absent from current observation does not mean that it never existed. Even when facts or history remain, if the route to them, the means of collation, or the circuit through which meaning can return is lost, a Meaning Loop can no longer close in the present field.

### 4.2 Cutoff and Accumulation

Null is first of all a break in connection.

The breaking of connection, however, does not necessarily erase the history that had been connected. History that has lost its pathway, words that were never received, gratitude that was never returned, harm that was never processed, and possibilities that were not selected may remain without being able to close as meaning.

Null may therefore be accompanied by a layer of accumulation.

> Null accumulates until meaning can close.

This expression does not mean that Null becomes an active agent of preservation. It means that histories deprived of Return Paths do not simply disappear and therefore accumulate as Residuals because their Meaning Loops cannot close.

Literature can work with this accumulation.

An unspoken past distorts present action. An event believed forgotten returns through repetition. The absence of someone whose name has been lost continues to determine the arrangement of a community. A discomfort that remains unclosed even as the plot advances later becomes an entrance into another history.

Null in literature is not decorative blankness.

It is an operational structure indicating where connection has been lost and what continues to accumulate without closure.

### 4.3 Fiction, Lies, Forgetting, Silence, Secrets, and Blank Space

Fiction, lies, forgetting, silence, secrets, and blank space are not themselves Null. Each operates differently in relation to historical connection.

| Item | Relation to Null |
|---|---|
| Fiction | may leave a direct route and reconnect to history or a truth layer through another route |
| Lie | may disguise a connection and cause a false Meaning Loop to form |
| Forgetting | may remove reachability to history without necessarily erasing the history itself |
| Silence | may refuse, hold, or protect connection; it is not automatically cutoff |
| Secret | limits who may connect and under what conditions; it can protect history or monopolize it |
| Blank space | may open connection through the reader or conceal a missing history |

Fiction in general therefore cannot be identified with falsehood or Null.

Literary fiction can generate Null, disguise it, or create a Return Path into histories accumulated within it. What matters is not only what is factual, but which connections have been cut and which have been opened.

### 4.4 Reconnection Is Not Restoration

Even when a severed history becomes reconnectable, the prior state cannot necessarily be restored.

Remembering a forgotten event, discovering a lost record, or giving language to an experience that could not previously be spoken may restore connection. The period of cutoff, histories formed during that period, and what was not returned do not disappear.

The Return Path made by literature is not a complete restoration of the past.

It is a way of opening another possibility of communication from the present toward the past while retaining the cutoff itself, allowing unclosed meaning to pass into subsequent history.

## 5. Ku / Emptiness — Not a Coequal Item in World Setting

Ku / Emptiness is not Null.

Null is described within a world in which boundaries, history, communication, and meaning have already arisen, as the severing of historical connection. Ku points toward what precedes naming, comparison, and boundary.

Ku, however, is not properly an object that can simply be named.

Expressions such as "before naming," "undifferentiated," "pure possibility," or "primordial entity" are only provisional ports from post-structural language pointing toward Ku. Even the question of whether Ku is or is not identical to the thing-in-itself cannot simply be placed on an ordinary plane of comparison.

Comparison requires the cutting out of objects, axes of comparison, identity and difference, function or structure, and an observation surface. These operations already belong to a phase in which differentiation has advanced and boundaries have arisen.

This note therefore does not directly identify Ku with physical space, mathematical structure, a set of possible worlds, or a primordial state inside a literary work.

What an author can establish as setting belongs to a world after differentiation.

Literature can point toward Ku through silence before world-formation, a sense prior to naming, images of undifferentiation, or expressions that appear to precede boundary. Those expressions are not Ku itself.

In this note, Ku is therefore not one more item of worldmaking, but:

> the limit encountered when worldmaking attempts to speak about what precedes setting.

## 6. Operational Speeds of Literary Worlds

The character of a literary world is not determined only by what exists in it. Even worlds containing the same elements can make different forms of life possible if events arrive at different speeds, responsibility returns at different speeds, or meaning requires different amounts of time to close.

This note distinguishes at least the following speeds.

### 6.1 Causal Speed

The speed with which an action appears as a consequence.

In folktales, good and bad acts may return clear consequences within a short period. In long novels, a decision made by one generation may return to the next in forms invisible to the original actor. Worlds with rapid causality make action and consequence easier to connect, but may also approach the structure of a moralizing device. Worlds with slow causality can handle complexity closer to ordinary reality, but connections are more easily rendered invisible.

### 6.2 Communication Speed

The speed with which one subject's experience, words, pain, or intention reaches another subject.

This is not merely physical transmission speed. Words can arrive without meaning arriving, while silence may reach another person only after a long period.

In worlds with slow communication, misunderstanding and isolation form history. In worlds where communication is too fast, secrecy, maturation, and perception before language may become difficult to retain.

### 6.3 Responsibility-Return Speed

The speed with which burdens, harm, benefit, gratitude, or demands for response produced by an action return to the actor or community.

When responsibility returns extremely quickly, it easily takes the form of immediate retaliation or direct moral causality. When it returns extremely slowly, the actor may leave without observing consequences while the burden flows downstream onto another subject.

Literature can compress responsibility returns that in reality would exceed a single human lifespan. It can also delay the revelation of what remained unreturned behind responsibility that appeared to have been processed immediately.

### 6.4 Meaning-Closure Speed

The speed with which an event acquires understanding, interpretation, and a place within a narrative, forming a Meaning Loop.

Works with rapid meaning closure are often easier to read. They can also assign meaning too early and erase Residuals. In works with slower meaning closure, the reader must retain unresolved matters, contradictions, and pressure. The act of holding them becomes part of inhabiting the literary world.

The ending of a work does not need to close every Meaning Loop. What is closed and what remains open for later return is itself part of worldmaking.

## 7. Operational Ranges of Literary Worlds

As with speed, the range within which something can reach also determines the character of a literary world.

### 7.1 Effective Range of Action

How far one person's action can affect others, places, or periods.

Some works allow the decision of a hero to change an entire world; others leave only a small act of care within one room as a reliable change. The range of an action is not identical to the value of that action.

If range is drawn too broadly, the agency of others may be absorbed into a single protagonist. A narrower range can sometimes show how to participate without claiming ownership of the whole world.

### 7.2 Range of Observation

Whose experience and history the work allows to enter its description of the world.

Does the work refuse to narrate what a viewpoint character does not know? Does only the narrator know the whole? Does a peripheral viewpoint expose burdens invisible from the institutional center?

What is not observed is not necessarily nonexistent.

By reading the pressure with which the outside of the observational range remains in a work, one can examine what the world has backgrounded in order to arise.

### 7.3 Range of Historical Retention

In what and in whom events remain: memory, records, bodies, institutions, land, or objects.

An individual may forget while a community remembers. Something can disappear from official records while remaining in bodily response or land use. Conversely, records may be preserved while the lived temperature of those involved is lost.

Historical retention in a literary world is not the quantity of documentary material. It concerns how the past continues changing the reception and available actions of downstream subjects.

### 7.4 Range of Return Paths

Who can respond to whom? How far can a complaint or appeal reach? Are Return Paths available to the dead, the weak, the enemy, other species, future generations, or nonhuman existences?

In a world where only the center can decide response, forgiveness, punishment, or salvation, Return Paths are monopolized. Worlds with multiple Return Paths close more slowly and retain incompatible voices. The refusal to erase those Residuals can itself become an ethical condition of the world.

## 8. Provisional Descriptive Surfaces for Reading the Laws of a World

The preceding sections can be summarized as the following descriptive surfaces for reading literary works.

| Descriptive surface | Question |
|---|---|
| Conditions of existence | What can appear as an existence, event, or subject? |
| Truth | What resists narrative or institutional convenience, and what rupture remains? |
| Beauty | In what form do difference, relation, and movement become perceptible? |
| Goodness | How are resulting boundaries and histories handled, what is protected, and what is returned? |
| Null | Where is historical connection severed, and what accumulates without closure? |
| Causal speed | How long does an action take to appear as a consequence? |
| Communication speed | How long does experience or meaning take to reach another? |
| Responsibility-return speed | Where and when do the burdens or demands for response produced by action return? |
| Meaning-closure speed | How long does an event take to form a Meaning Loop? |
| Range of action | Whom and how far can one action change? |
| Range of observation | Whose experience enters the description of the world? |
| Range of historical retention | In what does the past remain, and whose downstream conditions does it alter? |
| Range of Return Paths | Who can respond to whom, object, or reconnect? |

These are not criteria for assigning scores to works.

Nor must every work fill every category. They are descriptive surfaces for reading which conditions are strongly established, which are deliberately left blank, and which remain as Residuals that even the work itself cannot determine.

Worldmaking is not limited to excellent literature.

Every work makes some form of world condition. What differs is which histories those conditions erase, which Return Paths they close, and which consequences remain invisible to author or reader. To read literature as worldmaking is not to praise it, but to make the made conditions and their consequences collatable.

## 9. Between Reality and Literature

A literary world is not a closed domain completely severed from reality.

Authors use languages, bodily sensations, institutions, memories, and narrative forms drawn from reality. Readers enter works carrying their own histories. Differences generated within a work can alter perception, judgment, vocabulary, and relations after reading.

In this sense, a work is both another world and a surface of contact capable of altering the downstream conditions of reality.

What literature makes possible, however, cannot necessarily be implemented directly in reality.

Literature can compress time, expand observational range, give voices to the dead, arrange contingency, and return responsibility into a single ending. In reality, one cannot read another person's interior from an omniscient viewpoint or fill a lost history by authorial authority.

When the worldmaking capacity of literature is carried into reality, differences in authority must be examined.

Moving a character inside a work differs from treating a real other person as a character in one's own narrative. Receiving one's own life literarily differs from absorbing another person's history into one's own story of growth.

Literature can therefore become not only a testing ground for freedom to make worlds, but also a testing ground for learning which domains must not be made on behalf of others in reality.

## 10. Failures and Residuals of Worldmaking

Worldmaking does not aim only at completing a perfectly consistent world.

There are at least two kinds of failure in a work.

One occurs when the work cannot retain the conditions it established and discards characters or histories for authorial convenience. This can be collated as inconsistency in worldmaking.

The other occurs when what the work attempted to handle cannot close within the form it prepared and appears as a Residual. Such a Residual is not necessarily a mere defect. It can become an entrance into later reading or making as a trace that the world exceeded the author's grasp.

Not explaining everything is different from concealing what cannot be explained.

Not resolving everything is also different from abandoning histories that the work itself generated.

The precision of literary worldmaking should therefore be read not only through the completeness of a closed world, but also through the position and Return Paths left for what could not be closed.

## 11. Provisional Propositions

The provisional propositions of this note are:

1. Literature makes not only characters and settings, but the conditions under which existence, observation, causality, communication, history, responsibility, and meaning can arise.
2. Literature is a forum in which a made world is opened to the observation of others, making its consequences collatable among author, work, and reader.
3. A work does not operate only as the author's property; the conditions it establishes generate downstream conditions beyond authorial intention.
4. Truth may concern proto-structure or pre-structure as that which resists the convenience of narration or observation, without thereby becoming directly grasped or possessed.
5. Beauty is a mode of making in which structured differences, relations, and movements acquire perceptible form on the side of observation.
6. Goodness is an operational mode concerned with how boundaries, subjects, histories, harm, and responsibility are preserved, returned, and passed onward after they arise.
7. Null is a state in which historical connection is severed and a Meaning Loop cannot form or persist; it is not simple nonexistence.
8. When histories deprived of connection do not disappear, Null can be accompanied by an accumulation layer of unclosed meaning and Residuals.
9. Fiction, lies, forgetting, silence, secrets, and blank space are not Null itself, but can become distinct operations that generate, disguise, hold, protect, bypass, or reconnect what relates to Null.
10. Ku is not a coequal item within a world setting, but the limit encountered when worldmaking attempts to speak of what precedes setting.
11. Literary worlds possess different operational structures according to the speeds of causality, communication, responsibility return, and meaning closure, and according to the ranges of action, observation, historical retention, and Return Paths.
12. Literature can alter the downstream conditions of reality, but authorial authority within a work cannot be applied to real others.

---

## 12. Limits of This Note and Future Work

This note remains at the stage of proposing a general model for describing literary worldmaking. It does not currently provide a formal model or empirical classification.

At minimum, future work should examine:

- how the unit of worldmaking differs among narrative literature, poetry, essays, drama, and documentary literature;
- how narrative techniques such as narrator, focalization, and free indirect discourse can be described through observational range and communication pathways;
- differences among genres in expected causal speed and meaning-closure speed;
- how reader communities, criticism, commensuration, and derivative works increase or reduce Return Paths in a literary world;
- concrete types of Null operation in literary works;
- the relation between factual sequence in reality and layers of narrative truth;
- comparison between literary worldmaking and worldmaking through institutions, scientific models, and interfaces; and
- verification and revision of the descriptive surfaces through close reading of individual works.

In particular, the axes of speed and range remain conceptual guide-lines at the present stage. Whether they can be treated as independent axes, and what dependencies arise among them from work to work, must be examined through concrete readings.

## Document Relation

This note serves as the general model of literary ontology within Scientific Ontology.

Where [`Narrative_Truth_and_Structural_Reading.en.md`](../Cross_Domain_Ontological_Notes/Narrative_Truth_and_Structural_Reading.en.md) primarily addresses how narrative rearranges factual sequences and makes layers of truth that are difficult to see in ordinary reality observable, this note addresses what conditions a work makes for a world and what consequences follow from those conditions.

Close-reading notes on individual works and structural descriptions of creative universes can serve as applications of this model. Such applications do not automatically verify the model. They should instead test its descriptive surfaces and, where necessary, lead to integration, division, or withdrawal of particular axes.
