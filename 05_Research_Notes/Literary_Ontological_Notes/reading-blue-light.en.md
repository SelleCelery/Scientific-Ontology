# Reading the Blue Light

## Miyazawa Kenji’s _Spring and Asura_ and History-Field Topology

> Status: Literary / Ontological Note

## Positioning

### Neither literary scholarship nor physical theory, but an ontological reading

This essay does not attempt to explain Miyazawa Kenji through science.

It also does not claim that _Spring and Asura_ proves History-Field Topology.

What I attempt here is more limited, and perhaps more dangerous in a different way. I read several phrases from the preface to Miyazawa Kenji’s _Spring and Asura_—the “phenomenon called I,” the “organic alternating-current lamp,” the “blue illumination,” and the “ghosts”—as structural images of selfhood, light, communication, history, and emptiness.

So this is not literary scholarship in the strict sense.  
It is not physics.  
It is not Buddhist studies.

It is a non-scientific study.

By “non-scientific,” I do not mean careless, anti-scientific, or arbitrary. I mean only that this essay does not aim at scientific proof. One can still read structures, compare forms, and trace conceptual pathways without claiming empirical verification.

History-Field Topology, as I use the term here, is an ontological model for reading selfhood and existence not as fixed substances, but as arrangements of history, connection, boundary, observation, severance, and reconnection.

An entity is not merely a point that exists “there.”  
It has history.  
It has boundaries.  
It connects to some things, becomes cut off from others, and sometimes reconnects in another form.  
Within that arrangement, selfhood, meaning, and relation arise.

I use this model to read Kenji.

Not in order to take authority away from him.  
Not in order to subordinate his poetry to my own framework.  
Rather, I want to see what kind of resonance his words return when placed beside this model.

To say it more sharply: I do not hand authority back to Kenji.

Nor do I hand it back to science.  
Nor to Buddhism, modern theory, physics, or AI.

I do not use them as authorities.  
I place them as structures.  
Then I read where they resonate.

## Why does Kenji not become obsolete?

### Scientific vocabulary, religion, body, cosmos, and luminous selfhood

Miyazawa Kenji does not seem to become obsolete.

Of course, part of this is institutional. He is taught in schools. His children’s stories continue to be read. Poems such as “Ame ni mo Makezu” are often received through moral or ethical imagery.

But that explanation is not enough.

Kenji’s language strangely holds together modern scientific vocabulary, Buddhist cosmology, bodily sensation, agriculture, minerals, light, the dead, and a self that seems to glow.

He does not write the world as a simple collection of matter.  
Nor does he write it merely as psychological projection.

The world shines, trembles, burns, muddies, flows, and communicates.  
The self is not a fixed subject, but a phenomenon.  
The mind is not a sealed inner box. It crosses with landscapes, galaxies, minerals, asuras, sea urchins, air, and saltwater.

That is why Kenji’s language still connects oddly well with contemporary networked subjectivity.

The modern self is also not a stable substance.

It glows, posts, streams, is observed, is misread, performs salvation, and becomes ghost-like. Accounts, icons, posting histories, notifications, flame wars, approval metrics, deleted posts, surviving screenshots: these flicker around the self.

This is not a claim of influence.  
Kenji did not predict internet culture.  
The point is structural similarity: a self that glows, is observed, becomes history, and leaves ghosts.

The reason Kenji still feels contemporary may be that he did not write the self as an object. He wrote it as a luminous event.

## The self-image in the preface to _Spring and Asura_

### The phenomenon called “I”

The beginning of the preface to _Spring and Asura_ is already unstable.

The “I” is not presented as a settled entity.  
The “I” is a phenomenon.

This is not merely modesty.  
It is a radical answer to the question: what is the self?

I am not a fixed point.  
I am happening.  
I am flickering.  
I arise between something and something else.

In the vocabulary of History-Field Topology, the self is a sustained communication pattern generated when external event-streams touch internal history through a boundary.

The self is not simply inside.  
The self is rendered through contact with the outside.  
It is observed, responds, reflects, and is observed again. Through this process, the self acquires history.

That is why the phrase “the phenomenon called I” is so strong.

It opens a path to reading the self not as an object, but as a flickering history-field.

## Organic alternating-current lamp

### Reading the self as a circuit

Kenji describes the self as something like an “organic alternating-current lamp.” English translations differ, but the phrase has become one of the most striking images in the preface. English translations of the preface render this electric, organic, flickering self in related ways, and the preface is widely treated as a representative entrance into _Spring and Asura_. ([明治学院大学](https://www.meijigakuin.ac.jp/gengobunka/bulletins/archive/pdf/2018/26Tomiyama_p72.pdf))

The force of this image is that the self is not only something that shines.  
It is something through which current passes.

A lamp is not simply an object sitting there.  
There is a circuit.  
There is current.  
There is resistance.  
There is emission.

Without connection, it does not shine.  
With the wrong connection, it burns out.

An organic alternating-current lamp is a self with a body, a flow, and conditions of conduction.

Read through History-Field Topology, this is close to treating the self as a boundary-circuit.

Something arrives from outside.  
It touches internal history.  
Resistance occurs.  
Ignition occurs.  
It shines.  
It flickers.  
That flicker becomes experience.

Here, the self is not a sealed interior.  
The self is a passage.

But it is not a defenseless passage.  
There are things it lets through, things it repels, things it transforms, and things that would burn it out.

This image is also contemporary.

Human beings receive information.  
But information does not become meaning by itself.  
Only when it touches history, encounters friction at a boundary, and passes through some kind of circuit does it become meaning.

The organic alternating-current lamp can therefore be read as an apparatus of meaning-generation.

## Blue illumination

### Qualia and the observational surface

In the preface to _Spring and Asura_, the “blue illumination” appears as a charged image. The Japanese text is available through Aozora Bunko, which preserves the public-domain text of Kenji’s work. ([青空文庫](https://www.aozora.gr.jp/cards/000081/files/1058_15403.html))

Blue is cool.  
It has distance.  
It is transparent, close to night, and also close to observation.  
It is not the heat of red.  
It is not the all-burning exposure of white.  
It is a color still flickering as it watches the world.

First, I want to read this blue illumination as a surface of experience.

When the external world touches internal history, the world does not remain mere data. It becomes felt. That “felt surface” appears as illumination.

In this sense, the blue illumination may be the observational surface where the world becomes experience.

From here, another reading opens: youth.

In Japanese, “youth” is often written with the characters for blue and spring. I do not claim that Kenji intended this. But spring and blue resonate.

Spring is generation.  
Blue is immaturity, beginning, distance, observation, and a still-outward light.

Youth is not simply a bright and pleasant season. It is the phase in which the self is not yet stable as an entity, but exposed to the world as a phenomenon, still testing what it can conduct, what it must repel, and what kind of light it can emit.

In this sense, _Spring and Asura_ can be read as a poem of youth.

Spring is generation.  
Asura is conflict.  
The blue illumination still shines outward.  
The ghosts are transparent.  
The circuit is alternating.

The blue illumination still lights the world.  
But that light is already wounded.

Perhaps that is why it is blue.

## Ghosts

### Historical afterimages and the communication field

Kenji’s “ghosts” do not seem to be merely dead persons.

They appear as transparent afterimages: things that pass through, compound, flicker.

Read through History-Field Topology, a ghost is not something that has simply disappeared. It is a history that has not fully closed.

Something that should have ended, but still remains in the communication field.  
Something left in someone’s words, bodily reactions, or the light of a scene.

It may not exist as a present fact.  
But as meaning, it still flows.

A ghost is an unresolved history.  
Or a history still circulating through another circuit.

This reading also connects with my own poem:

“The ghost contained in that flickering circuit may be passing through you and through me.”

Here too, the ghost is not simply a dead spirit. It is a history that flows in. It passes through you, passes through me, shines somewhere, becomes a blade somewhere, and elsewhere is expanded as an information circuit.

A ghost need not be erased.  
But one must not be ruled by it either.

Let the ghost pass.  
Repel the blade.

This distinction becomes an ethics of connection.

## Emptiness, dependent arising, and communication

### Not fixed substance, but relational arising

It is difficult to think about Kenji without Buddhism, especially the Lotus Sutra. Kenji’s relation to the Lotus Sutra has long been treated as a major axis for interpreting his work. ([J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/ibk1952/44/2/44_2_772/_article))

Still, I do not borrow Kenji’s faith as an answer.  
Nor do I reduce Buddhism to History-Field Topology.

I only want to look at structural resemblance.

Emptiness is not mere nothingness.  
It does not simply mean that nothing exists.  
It points, rather, toward the impossibility of grasping things as fixed, self-existing substances.

Dependent arising means that things arise through relation, not as isolated entities.

This resonates with History-Field Topology.

In History-Field Topology, existence is not an isolated point.  
It has history, boundary, and meaning through connection.

Observation is not merely looking at an object from outside. It is contact with the object’s history, and that contact changes the observer’s history as well.

Meaning arises as a temporarily closed communication loop within a history.

In this sense, emptiness approaches the idea that something cannot be closed as a fixed substance.

And from here, emptiness can play with the metaphor of a boson network.

This boson network is not a physical claim.  
I am not saying that Kenji was talking about boson networks.  
I am not saying Buddhism anticipated Feynman diagrams.

But as analogy, it can be played with.

A Feynman diagram gives us a way to think of particles not as little fixed balls, but through paths and interactions. Bosons appear as mediators of interaction. In History-Field Topology, existence arises through connection, path, synchronization, and severance rather than as isolated objects.

Kenji’s “phenomenon called I” is likewise not a fixed point. It connects with the world, shines, flickers, contains ghosts, and exists in the same field as air, saltwater, galaxies, asuras, and scenes.

So emptiness, Kenji, Feynman diagrams, and boson networks do not scientifically merge.

They can be structurally played together.

And this “play” matters.

Do not entrust correctness to science.  
Do not entrust correctness to religion.  
Do not entrust correctness to Kenji.  
Do not reassign correctness back to past authorities.

Instead, build bridges where structures resemble one another.  
See whether they can be crossed.  
If they cannot, return.  
If the bridge is dangerous, mark it as metaphor.

That is enough.

## Contemporary popular culture

### Luminous personas, performed salvation, and boundary failure

Contemporary popular culture repeatedly returns to figures who shine, perform, collapse, or are consumed by the gaze of others.

One reason Kenji does not become obsolete may be that he wrote the self as a luminous phenomenon.

In contemporary media culture, the self increasingly glows.

It posts.  
It streams.  
It becomes an icon.  
It becomes a character.  
It performs salvation.  
It shows wounds.  
It shows anger.  
It receives approval.  
It deletes.  
And yet, after deletion, traces remain.

This is ghost-like.

An account disappears, but screenshots remain.  
A statement is deleted, but history remains.  
A persona is performed, but the body performing it is exhausted.  
A luminous self exists by being seen, but can be damaged by being seen too much.

A very accessible global example is _Star Wars_. The “dark side” is often framed through fear, anger, hatred, and suffering, as in Yoda’s warning to Anakin. The official _Star Wars_ site summarizes the scene as one where Yoda senses great fear in the boy. ([StarWars.com](https://www.starwars.com/video/fear-is-the-path-to-the-dark-side))

Read through the present frame, the dark side is not merely “evil power.” It can be read as a boundary failure.

Something is taken in too deeply.  
Fear is not given distance.  
Attachment becomes possession.  
The self closes around loss.  
What should have remained a relation becomes an enclosed circuit.  
The more it tries to control the outside, the more the inside collapses into a closed loop.

This is not a claim about what _Star Wars_ “really means.” It is only a useful popular analogy. The dark side becomes intelligible as a failure of boundary design: too much intake, too little circulation, too much closure, and no safe way to return the excess to the world.

In that sense, a luminous or powerful self is not automatically healthy. A self that shines too much may burn. A self that absorbs too much may collapse. A self that refuses all connection may preserve itself, but at the cost of becoming unreadable.

This brings us back to Kenji.

Kenji did not predict streaming culture, online personas, or cinematic mythologies of inner collapse. But by writing the self as a phenomenon, a lamp, an illumination, and a haunted field, he created images that still speak to modern forms of selfhood.

## Caution

### Do not explain Kenji through science

Up to this point, we have crossed several dangerous bridges.

Organic alternating-current lamp.  
Blue illumination.  
Ghosts.  
Emptiness.  
Dependent arising.  
Feynman diagrams.  
Boson networks.  
Online personas.  
The dark side.

It is easy to place these words side by side.  
It is dangerous to pretend that placing them side by side proves anything.

This essay does not explain Miyazawa Kenji through science.  
It does not use Kenji’s poetry as evidence for History-Field Topology.  
It does not explain Buddhism through physics.  
It does not translate Feynman diagrams into literary interpretation.

What it does is structural reading.

It is not a scientific claim.  
It is not orthodox literary commentary.  
It is not religious studies.

It records that Kenji’s language contains, with remarkable density, the problems of self, light, ghosts, emptiness, and connection. It also records that this density resonates strongly with the vocabulary of History-Field Topology.

The important thing is not to deposit correctness somewhere else.

I do not say this is correct because Kenji said it.  
I do not say it is correct because it resembles Buddhism.  
I do not say it is correct because it resembles scientific vocabulary.  
I do not say it is correct because it also connects with modern media culture.

The structures resemble one another.

So I read.  
So I play.  
So I build bridges.

That attitude itself is non-scientific study.

## Conclusion

### Structural reading as non-scientific study

The preface to Miyazawa Kenji’s _Spring and Asura_ appears to me as a powerful entrance into reading the self as a luminous history-field.

The “phenomenon called I” is not a fixed entity, but a self that is happening.  
The “organic alternating-current lamp” is the self as a circuit between outside and inside.  
The “blue illumination” is qualia igniting at the surface of observation.  
The “ghosts” are historical afterimages remaining in the communication field.

Buddhist emptiness and dependent arising resonate as a world-image in which things do not stand as fixed substances, but arise through relation. Feynman diagrams and boson networks do not prove this scientifically; they provide analogies for thinking about connection, path, and interaction.

And there may be something beyond this.

If _Spring and Asura_ can be read as youth through the blue illumination, then the seasons have not ended.

There is summer.  
There is vermilion summer.  
There is red illumination.  
There are hungry ghosts.  
There is hunger, excess, proof, anger, and vital reaction.

There may also be white autumn and black winter.  
There may be white illumination.  
There may be a black hole.

But maturity is not simply becoming black.

Maturity may be learning not to confuse separation with disposal.  
It may be learning that not bringing something back immediately is not betrayal.  
It may be learning that to preserve a connection does not always mean keeping it nearby. Sometimes it means allowing it to enter a long circulation.

There is an old Japanese poem:

“Even if divided, I believe we shall meet again at the end.”

Separate, yet meet later.  
Do not bring it back at once.  
Do not close it at once.  
Do not understand it at once.  
And yet, do not discard it completely.

Perhaps being able to place that poem inside the problem of connection is one form of maturity.

From Kenji’s blue illumination to a red illumination.  
From red to white.  
From white to black.  
And even after becoming black, to let the circulation of ghosts continue.

This is not orthodox Kenji scholarship.  
It is not science.

But it is a non-scientific study for reading self, light, ghosts, emptiness, and connection.

And I will probably continue this kind of study.
