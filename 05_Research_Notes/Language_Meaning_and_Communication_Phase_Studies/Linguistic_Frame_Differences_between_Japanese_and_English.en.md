## 0. Position

Stating the same content in Japanese and English does not necessarily reproduce the same semantic movement.

Grammatical frames do not merely give different outer forms to completed meaning. They commit the text to subjects, objects, tense, givenness, responsibility, degree of assertion, and relational distance at different points and in different forms.

The central question is:

> How do differences in the grammatical and discourse resources available in Japanese and English affect the timing of segmentation of whole pressure, the possibility of suspension, responsibility allocation, dependence on shared history, and residuals produced by commensuration?

## 1. Central Thesis

Japanese often has resources such as argument omission, topicalization, sentence-final modality, relational grammar, and shared history that can leave parts of agency, judgment, and responsibility underdetermined until the end of the sentence or across the broader discourse.

English often requires explicit argument structure, a subject and finite verb, articles and number, and explicit relations between clauses at comparatively early points, which can fix agent, object, givenness, and logical relation earlier in the sentence.

This is not a fixed essence of either language. Genre, spoken or written register, speaker proficiency, style, translation policy, and institutional setting can alter these tendencies substantially.

This note proposes the following working hypothesis:

> Japanese-English differences should be read not as a difference in which language is more logical, but as differences in the allocation of semantic commitment: which relations are fixed at which points, and what can remain entrusted to the reader's history and discourse context.

## 2. Contrastive Axes

| Axis | Resources often available in Japanese | Resources often available in English | Question for commensuration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Topic / subject | Topic marking with `wa`, zero anaphora, argument omission | Explicit subjects, pronouns, argument structure | Whom did the commensurator add as subject? |
| Timing of judgment | Sentence-final predicates, auxiliaries, final particles | Earlier presentation of main-clause skeleton and finite verb | Was a suspended judgment closed too early? |
| Givenness | Often entrusted to context and shared history | Articles, singular/plural, demonstratives | What was made given or individuated? |
| Responsibility allocation | Subjectless expressions, passive, state description, discourse inference | Agentive subjects, causal clauses | Was an agent of responsibility added or erased? |
| Relation marking | Honorifics, forms of address, sentence-final forms, implicit distance | Pronouns, modals, politeness strategies | Can power and distance be preserved in another form? |
| Connection | Juxtaposition, conjunctive particles, contextual dependence | Conjunctions, subordinate clauses, explicit logical markers | Was an ambiguous relation fixed into causality? |
| Continuity of pressure | Long modification, sentence-final recovery, aftereffect | Sentence splitting, front-loaded claims | Did splitting destroy the continuity of whole pressure? |

## 3. Topic and Agent

In Japanese, a topic is not necessarily the acting agent. Arguments predictable from conversation are also frequently omitted.

```text
この制度では、判断できない。
```

This sentence leaves open who cannot make a judgment, whether the system itself is incapable of judgment, or whether people within the system are prevented from judging.

English requires choices such as:

```text
This system does not allow users to make a judgment.
No reliable judgment can be made under this system.
Decision-making becomes impossible within this institutional arrangement.
```

All can be natural, but they allocate agency, capacity, and responsibility differently.

When a commensurator supplies a subject, they are not simply filling a missing item. They are selecting one responsibility allocation from among possibilities left suspended in the source.

## 4. Sentence End and Timing of Commitment

Japanese can accumulate modification, conditions, reasons, quotation, and qualification before closing the judgment at the sentence end.

```text
履歴が保存されず、生成過程も評価されず、現場からの返路が細い社会では、自己演出が能力として扱われやすくなる。
```

A comparable subordinate structure can be built in English, but long sentences may lead English readers to expect the main clause earlier, making sentence splitting more natural.

Splitting improves readability. It can, however, weaken the felt convergence of multiple conditions as one pressure into a single conclusion.

Sentence splitting therefore requires an audit not only of information preservation but also of continuity of pressure.

## 5. Omission and Shared History

Argument omission in Japanese is not merely absence. It can be supported by discourse continuity, shared knowledge among participants, centers of attention, and relational distance.

Omission saves bandwidth and can keep multiple candidate subjects in suspension. For readers without the shared history, readers from another culture, AI systems, or machine translation, however, the referent may become unclear.

Supplying arguments in English improves readability but produces residuals such as:

- plurality of possible agents;
- structures in which no single agent exists;
- a state in which the speaker has not yet fixed responsibility;
- and the relational effect of deliberately not saying something.

## 6. Articles, Number, and Individuation

English often requires noun phrases to be marked as singular or plural and as definite or indefinite.

Japanese terms such as `境界`, `履歴`, and `意味` can move by context among a general concept, one particular boundary or history, and multiple boundaries or histories.

Choosing among `a boundary`, `the boundary`, `boundaries`, and `boundary` changes individuation and givenness.

Commensuration must therefore inspect not only lexical meaning but whether the concept is functioning in a given sentence as an individual, class, operation, or relation.

## 7. Relation and Honorifics

Japanese honorifics, forms of address, and sentence-final forms can encode distance, role, and authority between speakers together with propositional content.

English also has politeness, indirectness, modals, titles, and register, but does not necessarily encode the same relational structure at the same density.

Simply replacing polite Japanese with polite English may erase institutional subordination, cold distance, excessive formality, or refusal of intimacy.

Conversely, returning direct English subject-verb structures literally to Japanese can make them sound more assertive, aggressive, or self-centered than required.

## 8. Different Segmentation of the Same Pressure

Consider:

> 未解決のものを、未解決として残す。

Possible English renderings include:

```text
Leave unresolved matters unresolved.
Preserve what remains unresolved as unresolved.
Do not treat unresolved matters as resolved.
```

The first is concise but can sound like abandonment. The second foregrounds preservation but is stiff. The third explicitly resists false closure, but turns the quiet preservation of the Japanese source into a prohibition.

The choice should therefore be made according to what the document needs to preserve, and the differences lost in the chosen rendering should be recorded.

## 9. Using Japanese-English Round Trips as a Collational Instrument

Passing a Japanese authoritative text into English tends to expose:

- omitted agents;
- whether a relation is causality or juxtaposition;
- singular versus plural;
- definition versus operation;
- given versus new;
- hypothesis versus assertion.

Returning English into Japanese tends to expose:

- whether subjects have been fixed too strongly;
- whether propositions have been fragmented excessively;
- whether relations or aftereffects have been lost;
- whether explicit logic has added a pressure absent from the source.

This round trip does not treat either language as the correct mold. It uses different grammatical frames to observe segmentation possibilities present in the source pressure.

## 10. Counterexamples and Variables

The contrastive axes in this note are hypotheses about tendencies and explicitly contain counterexamples.

English can suspend agency, responsibility, and judgment through passive constructions, nominalization, impersonal constructions, long embeddings, hedging, and discourse-level delay.

Japanese can fix agent and judgment early through explicit subjects, assertive predicates, short imperatives, legal language, specifications, and newspaper headlines.

Within the same language, large differences can arise from:

- conversation, academic writing, law, literature, social media, and UI;
- native speakers, second-language speakers, and translators;
- individual style, professional education, and organizational norms;
- spoken versus written, synchronous versus asynchronous communication;
- human generation, AI generation, and collaborative editing.

Japanese-English differences should therefore be treated not as essences attached to language names but as distributions of available resources and the grammatical/discourse operations selected in a given setting.

## 11. Japanese-English Differences in AI

In multilingual LLMs, language choice has been reported to affect not only surface output but also performance, token efficiency, internal representations, language mixing, and knowledge activation.

Results vary, however, by model, task, training resources, and writing system. It cannot be generalized that a model always translates internally into English before reasoning, nor that it performs completely separate thought in each language.

From the perspective of Scientific Ontology, the following limited hypothesis is proposed:

> Input and generation language can alter the distribution of available grammatical frames, token segmentation, training history, and contextual constraints, thereby changing which semantic path is realized from the same question.

This does not claim that AI possesses a different human-like subjectivity for each language.

## 12. Collation with External Research

### 12.1 Zero Anaphora and Argument Omission

Research on Japanese zero anaphora shows that context, discourse centers, pragmatics, and semantic constraints participate in recovering unexpressed arguments. This contacts the present model's dependence on shared history.

Some accounts do not treat every omission as the deletion of an internally complete argument. The explanatory status of “omission” itself should therefore not be fixed prematurely.

### 12.2 Contrastive Discourse Research

Research on Japanese-English organization of text, stance, engagement, and genre differences provides a point of collation for extending grammatical comparison beyond isolated sentences into discourse and genre.

### 12.3 Multilingual LLMs

Recent research reports language-specific local structures alongside shared representations, differences in reasoning paths associated with language choice, language mixing, and disparities involving low-resource languages. This note does not use such work as evidence for an essence of Japanese and English; it uses it as an empirical collation point for the possibility that grammatical frames affect generation paths.

## 13. Failure Modes

1. Fixing Japanese as ambiguous and English as logical.
2. Inferring speaker personality or culture from grammar.
3. Evaluating omission as information loss and explicitness as one-way improvement.
4. Generalizing from one genre or example sentence to the entire language.
5. Reverse-importing subjects supplied during English commensuration as the true intention of the Japanese source.
6. Identifying AI language differences with human linguistic cognition.

## 15. Definitional Residues

- Grouping diverse varieties under the names Japanese and English backgrounds dialect, style, professional domain, and historical differences.
- The commitment/suspension axis is useful, but English has many methods of suspension and Japanese many methods of early commitment.
- Differences in grammatical frames cannot be directly identified with differences in cognitive process.
- Commensuration examples are context-dependent and do not provide a generally optimal translation.

## 16. Non-Claim Boundary

- Japanese is not essentialized as more high-context, deeper, or more ambiguous than English.
- English is not claimed to be more logical or scientific than Japanese.
- National character, cognitive ability, or ethical character are not derived from grammatical differences.
- Findings from contrastive linguistics are not used as proof of Scientific Ontology.
- Multilingual LLM behavior is not transferred directly to human language cognition.
