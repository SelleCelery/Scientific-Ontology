## 0. Position

If meaning is fixed as a set of attributes located inside a word, the history and return paths of meaning formation become difficult to see.

Conversely, if related objects and propositions are added to extension without limit, the distinction between association and grounding is lost.

This note describes **collational intension** and **return-path extension** as, respectively, the inside of a collational movement and the domain into which that movement expands through other proposition groups while retaining a route back to the original proposition or collating subject.

## 1. Central Thesis

> To understand meaning is for a movement of the collating subject to pass through a proposition and form a round trip back from that proposition to the collating subject.
>
> The range of semantic elements retained and mutually collated within this round-trip movement is called **collational intension**.
>
> When the movement expands into other proposition groups and forms a return path that brings their differences back to the original proposition or collating subject, the range of that connection is called **return-path extension**.
>
> When a connection to another proposition is used as explanation, proof, grounding, or authority for the original proposition without forming such a return path, it is called **false extension**.

## 2. Relation to Standard Semantics

In formal semantics, intension may be treated as a function that yields an extension across possible worlds, while extension may refer to an object, truth value, set, or other denotation associated with an expression.

This note does not reject those technical definitions.

What it provides is an operational rereading for the linguistic research of Scientific Ontology, intended to audit concept understanding, citation, commensuration, and AI association.

The qualifiers `collational` and `return-path` are therefore retained in the formal terms. Where the shorter forms `intension` and `extension` appear below, they are document-local abbreviations and do not replace the terms of standard semantics.

## 3. Collational Intension

Collational intension is not a static list of attributes stored inside a proposition.

It is a bundle of semantic elements that mutually constrain one another within a movement that passes through a proposition and returns, and that are necessary for the movement to return to the original proposition.

For example, understanding “nursing” may involve treatment, observation, body, daily life, environmental adjustment, support for recovery, patient judgment, institutions, family, and sustainability.

Merely listing these items does not constitute intension.

Each must be able to return to nursing through relations such as: “adjusting the environment creates conditions for recovery without depriving the person of function.”

Intension is formed not by the number of included terms but by the relational density through which they can return to the original proposition.

## 4. Return-Path Extension

A movement can expand from proposition A into other domains B, C, and D.

“Nursing” may extend toward social design, organizational operation, AI support, and environmental adjustment.

For such movement to count as extension in the present sense, the following are required:

1. It must be possible to state what was preserved in the move.
2. The specific conditions of the destination must be examined.
3. The destination must not be identified with the original proposition.
4. Differences obtained at the destination must return to the original proposition.
5. The returned differences must be able to modify or limit the intension of the original proposition.

Extension is therefore not a list of objects. It is a connected domain with a return path.

## 5. Extension Return-Path Test

Before using a connection as an extension, ask:

| Question | If Yes | If No |
|---|---|---|
| Can you explain what was preserved from the original proposition when moving to the destination? | Candidate connection | Possibly mere association |
| Can you describe the destination's specific conditions? | Commensuration may be possible | Risk of false identity |
| Can differences return from the destination to the original proposition? | Candidate extension | One-way use |
| Can the original proposition be revised? | Collational relation | Risk of borrowed authority |
| Were non-commensurable residuals recorded? | Residual preservation | Risk of false closure |

Not every answer must be Yes. What matters is not to conceal a No while using the connection as grounding.

## 6. False Extension

False extension is not merely an error of relevance.

A real relation may exist. The problem arises when the connected destination has no return path and only its authority, fame, numbers, or impression is used to support the original proposition.

### 6.1 Borrowed-Authority Type

“A famous thinker said something similar, therefore this claim is correct.”

The text does not show at which proposition the correspondence holds or where it diverges.

### 6.2 Lexical-Co-Occurrence Type

Two domains use the same word, so they are treated as the same concept.

Terms such as “entropy,” “gravity,” and “field” are especially vulnerable when disciplinary definitions are erased.

### 6.3 Similarity-Metaphor Type

A structural resemblance is used to jump to identity of real mechanisms.

### 6.4 Decorative-Citation Type

A citation is attached as an external shell of authority without changing the inference in the text.

### 6.5 AI-Association Type

AI produces many related references and concepts but does not show a route back to the original proposition.

### 6.6 Market/Evaluation Type

Popularity, price, adoption, or search ranking is transferred into correctness of content.

## 7. Literature Is a Point of Collation, Not Automatically Grounding

Sources and scholarship can ground empirical facts, historical facts, or interpretations of a source when used according to the procedures of their respective fields.

For conceptual claims specific to Scientific Ontology, however, citation alone does not automatically provide grounding. Such references are used as points of collation that open possible readings and differences rather than as borrowed authority.

Rather than saying, “this source proves the claim,” the question is:

> If this source is read in this way, does a different structure concerning boundary, history, return path, or residual become visible?

Such a reading must distinguish the source text, its established context, and the extended reading.

Opening a text is different from appropriating it into one's own theory.

Existing interpretive communities may experience the creation of new connection points as intrusion. The reasons for rejection can be understood, but those reasons do not themselves prove that the reading fails.

## 8. False Extension and LLMs

LLMs can easily move into other propositions through high-dimensional associations among vocabulary, syntax, literature, and concepts.

This makes them strong at exploring extensions, but without return-path auditing they can mass-produce false extensions.

When auditing AI output, ask:

- What is the destination of the connection?
- Why was it connected?
- How does it return to the original proposition?
- How is the original proposition revised by the connection?
- What residual cannot be connected?
- Is a source functioning as evidence for an empirical or historical fact, a conceptual point of collation, or decorative authority?

“Related to” and “explains” are not the same relation.

## 9. Collation with External Research

### 9.1 Peirce

Peirce's semiotics, in which an interpretant can generate a further sign as a translation or development of the sign, provides a point of collation for thinking about semantic movement that expands into other propositions and returns into further interpretation.

Collational intension and return-path extension are not identified with Peirce's classifications.

### 9.2 Saussure

The view that semantic value arises through differences within a system provides a basis for reading intension as relational structure rather than isolated attributes.

This note additionally addresses how systems themselves change through history and return paths.

### 9.3 Eco

Encyclopedic semantic networks, the Model Reader, and the distinction between interpretation and use of a text provide important points of collation for considering the openness and limits of extension.

False extension contacts the condition in which a text or proposition is used one-way for one's own purposes without receiving constraints on the way back.

## 10. Failure Modes

1. **Attribute-box model**: reducing intension to a list of attributes.
2. **Object-list model**: reducing extension to a list of applicable examples.
3. **Unlimited association**: treating everything connectable as an extension.
4. **Borrowed authority**: using literature or scholarship as grounding without a return path.
5. **Source appropriation**: presenting an extended reading as the sole meaning of the original.
6. **Replacement of formal semantics**: treating this operational rereading as a substitute for standard definitions.
7. **Trust in AI enumeration**: mistaking a large number of associations for explanatory force.

## 11. Local Uses of High-Density Terms

| Term | Local use in this document | Not identical to | Definitional residual |
|---|---|---|---|
| Collational intension | Relational range of semantic elements preserved within a collational round trip through a proposition | Intension in formal semantics generally, attribute lists | Possible-world semantics, psychological concept representation |
| Return-path extension | Connected range that expands into other proposition groups and retains a return path to the original proposition | Extension in general, an object set alone | Empirical determination of applicable objects |
| False extension | Using a related destination as explanation, proof, or authority without a return path | Mere error, all free association | Difference between deliberate manipulation and unconscious conflation |
| Grounding of SO-specific claims | A relation that withstands source resistance, difference, return paths, and counterexamples rather than being settled by citation alone | Famous names, citation counts, popularity | Field-specific procedures for empirical, historical, and legal evidence |

## 12. Definitional Residues

- Redescribing intension and extension as movements does not address the precise denotation and truth conditions treated by formal semantics.
- Emphasizing return paths can narrow the treatment of irreversible effects, the dead, or past documents from which no direct reply can return. Return paths mediated by history, works, and subsequent action must also be included.
- The boundary between false extension and creative leap can change if a return path is later established.
- How far extended readings of literature should respect the boundaries of an existing field cannot be decided by a general rule alone.

## 13. Non-Claim Boundary

- This note does not claim that intension/extension in standard logic or formal semantics is mistaken.
- The truth or falsity of an interpretation is not determined by the presence of a return path alone.
- Existing scholarship is not subordinated as evidence for Scientific Ontology.
- Breadth of extension is not treated as evidence of theoretical superiority.
- An AI's ability to verbalize a return path does not prove actual understanding.

## 14. Unresolved Questions / Depth Interface

- How far should the minimum conditions of the extension return-path test be fixed?
- For traces from which no direct reply from the original addressee is possible, how should the conditions of a history-mediated return path be described?
- How should cases be recorded in which a false extension later becomes a genuine extension?
- How should rejection of interpretation be distinguished from defensive gatekeeping?
- Can detection of false extension by AI be operationalized as an audit?
